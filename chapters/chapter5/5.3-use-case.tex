\section{Multi-Vertical Use Case in \acs{E2E} Adaptive QoS Scenario}
\label{chap:results:use-case}

The experiments presented in Section~\ref{chap:results:exposure} evaluated the interaction model between the network application and the \acs{5G} network exposure interfaces provided by the testbed. However, these experiments focused on the performance of the exposure interfaces themselves, without assessing their impact on the underlying network or on other services sharing its resources.

This section addresses this gap by evaluating the vertical network application adaptive \acs{QoS} scenario introduced in Section~\ref{chap:impl:use-case}. The experiment assesses how the \acs{NEF} and CAMARA \acsp{API} implemented in this dissertation support adaptive \acs{QoS} management in the live \acs{5G} network at 5GAIner. More specifically, it measures the time required to enforce a new \acs{QoS} profile on the network. It then uses the developed passive network monitoring mechanisms to assess whether the resulting network conditions match the characteristics defined for the network slice and, subsequently, those of the newly enforced \acs{QoS} profile. Finally, it evaluates the impact of this change on the \acs{QoE} of other users sharing the network, using the \acs{FPS} of a video streaming application.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{chap:results:use-case:setup}

Given the mission-critical nature of \acs{PPDR} communications, sufficient network resources must be available to support their services whenever an emergency occurs. However, \acs{PPDR} events are sporadic and difficult to predict, making the permanent reservation of dedicated network resources costly and inefficient, due to long periods of low demand. For this reason, in real deployments, \acs{PPDR} and civilian traffic may end up sharing the same pool of network resources, even when served by separate network slices~\cite{CCAMPPDRDynamicResourceAllocation}.

Nevertheless, network applications, such as the one developed in this dissertation, can interact with the \acs{5G} network through the \acs{NEF} and CAMARA interfaces to dynamically request additional bandwidth for their streams whenever a \acs{PPDR} event occurs (e.g. car crash detected) or whenever the throughput of the inference video stream drops below a predefined threshold, as reported by the analytics capabilities of those same interfaces. Conversely, the application could request that the bandwidth available to civilian \acsp{UE} be temporarily restricted to prioritize mission-critical communications and ensure sufficient network resources for emergency services.

To reproduce this scenario, a streaming pipeline was developed to generate traffic and consume \acs{5G} network resources, as would be the case with civilian communications in real-world deployments. For this, a computer linked to a modem connected to the \acs{5G} network was used as the civilian \acs{UE}, streaming a video at 4K (3840x2160) 30 \acs{FPS} to put load on the network. On the \acs{UE}, a MediaMTX\footnote{\url{https://github.com/bluenviron/mediamtx}} streaming server was deployed as a Docker container, to which FFmpeg\footnote{\url{https://ffmpeg.org}} published the H.265 video stream. The stream was then consumed over \acs{RTSP}, using \acs{TCP} as transport, by another \acs{VM}, running an Ubuntu 22.04 \acs{VM} with 4 vCPUs, 8 GB \acs{RAM}, and 64 GB disk. To ensure that the stream traversed the \acs{5G} network, a static route was configured on the \acs{UE} to direct all traffic destined for the OpenStack \acs{VM} subnet through the modem interface.

To establish the network conditions under which the civilian streaming scenario will be evaluated, the following connectivity requirements were defined (Table~\ref{tab:results:use-case:qos}), each aimed at simulating a different \acs{QoS} environment.

In particular, a dedicated network slice was designed to support the delivery of high-resolution content, with moderate latency. The throughput requirements were derived from TR 26.925~\cite{TR26925}, which highlights typical traffic characteristics of media services on \acs{3GPP} networks. According to this report, 4K UHD video streams typically require between 5 and 25~Mbps. It also reports real use cases of 4K video encoded with H.265 (HEVC) at 30~\acs{FPS} requiring between 10-18~Mbps on average, with caps between 15 and 25~Mbps. Moreover, given the advertised average bitrate for the video stream file is around 13.12~Mbps, the throughput per \acs{UE} was bounded between 15 and 20~Mbps. Therefore, the slice should provide sufficient uplink capacity to support the stream originating at the \acs{UE}.

Although each network slice is designed to meet specific latency and throughput requirements, it should be emphasized that the \acs{5G} network in the 5GAIner infrastructure operates on a best-effort basis. This means that there are no strict resource guarantees within a network slice and, that the performance metrics observed in \ac{UE} traffic flows may fall below or exceed the limits of the specified \acsp{SLA}. This characteristic has also shaped the design of the proposed test case. In particular, under contention, a \acs{QoS} upgrade does not guarantee improved network conditions for the affected \acs{UE}. By contrast, a \acs{QoS} downgrade, such as the one presented in Table~\ref{tab:results:use-case:qos}, imposes a hard cap on the maximum uplink throughput of the \acs{UE} and is enforced by the network regardless of competing traffic. For this reason, the experiments focus on downgrading the \acs{QoS} profile of the civilian \acs{UE} to 4~Mbps, rather than upgrading that of the \acs{PPDR} \acs{UE}. This approach provides clear and measurable evidence of whether, and when, the requested \acs{QoS} profile is enforced by the network.

Two experiments were conducted to evaluate the adaptive \acs{QoS} use case, both using the same baseline environment described in the previous sections. This environment comprises the commercial-grade standalone \acs{5G} network deployed at \acs{ITAv} and its proprietary Slice Manager. Unlike the previous experiments, these experiments exercise the complete experimental setup illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:results:experimentalsetup}. The exposure interfaces and the network application are deployed on the OpenSlice \acs{VM} within the K3s cluster, whereas the \acs{UE}, i.e. a computer connected to a \acs{5G} modem, transmits uplink traffic to a separate \acs{VM}.

In the first experiment, this traffic is generated with iperf3\footnote{\url{https://iperf.fr}}, which sends constant \acs{UDP} traffic from the \acs{UE} to an iperf3 server hosted on that \acs{VM}. In the second experiment, the \acs{UE} instead streams the 4K video at 30~\acs{FPS} to the same \acs{VM}. Moreover, throughout both experiments, the testbed passive monitoring mechanisms, encompassing the packet and bytes exporter and the Prometheus \acs{TSDB} collect per-\acs{UE}/slice uplink and downlink throughput metrics. In the second experiment, these metrics are complemented by the \acs{RTT} measurements of the active \acs{ICMP} prober, also deployed on the K3s cluster.

\subsection{Procedure}
\label{chap:results:use-case:procedure}

Before both experiments, the network slice, the civilian \acs{UE}, the exposure interfaces and the network application were provisioned through the \acs{OSS}. All of these services were included in the same service order, following the service delivery process implemented in Section~\ref{chap:results:delivery}. Within this order, the \acs{CFS} characteristics pertaining to the network slice were configured according to the \acs{NEST} attributes listed in Table~\ref{tab:results:use-case:qos}. Similarly, the \acs{IMSI} of the SIM card inserted in the modem was also supplied, as well as the maximum uplink throughput for the \acs{UE} association, also with a value of 20~Mbps. The slice identifier is automatically inherited from the network slice service in the same order.

In addition, the specified \acs{QoS} requirements were supplied to the \acs{NEF} through the same order, as a static configuration file defining the \acs{QoS} profiles that can be requested through the \textit{AsSessionWithQoS} \acs{API}. These include a default profile, with 20~Mbps maximum bitrate matching the maximum uplink throughput per \acs{UE} defined in the \acs{NEST}, and the downgrade profile, limited to a max of 4~Mbps. The same configuration enabled the Huawei driver, described in Section~\ref{chap:impl:exposure:nef}, allowing the requested \acs{QoS} to be applied to the \acs{5G} network through the Slice Manager \acs{API}. Furthermore, the AnalyticsExposure \acs{API} was configured to query Prometheus as described in Section~\ref{chap:impl:exposure:nef}, enabling it to retrieve analytics from metrics collected in the actual network, through the bytes exporter. Together, these configurations allow \acs{NEF}Sim to operate in conjunction with a real network rather than in its default simulation mode, which represents one of the main contributions of this dissertation. In this setting, the \acs{NEF}Sim can enforce the requested \acs{QoS} profiles on the network and monitor its performance through measurements collected from the live \acs{5G} environment.

As described in Section~\ref{chap:impl:resource_lifecycle}, the resulting slice and \acs{UE} resources are fulfilled by the Slicing Generic Controller, which consumes the Slice Manager \acs{API} to instantiate the slice across the \acs{RAN} and \acs{CN} and to associate the \acs{UE} with it. Once the association was completed, the modem attached to the network slice obtained an \acs{IP} address attributed by the \acs{5G} network. Following provisioning, the \acs{NEF} database, specifically its \acs{UE} repository, was populated using a dedicated script with the modem's \acs{IMSI} and assigned \acs{IP} address. These identifiers matched those of the \acs{UE} provisioned in the network, ensuring that the requests received by the \acs{NEF} refer to the \acs{UE} attached to the slice.

With the environment provisioned, the two experiments were executed. The first experiment focused exclusively on the network side, evaluating whether network \acs{QoS} parameters could be modified on demand before investigating the impact of such modifications on application behaviour. Accordingly, the experiment used only the provisioned slice and \acs{UE}, while the exposure interfaces and the network application remained out of scope. In this test case, the \acs{UE} transmitted constant \acs{UDP} traffic to an iperf3 server for 120s at a rate of 15~Mbps, matching to the minimum uplink throughput per \acs{UE} defined in the \acs{NEST}. The datagram size was set to 1400~bytes, providing a relatively large payload while remaining below the threshold for \acs{IP} fragmentation. The throughput received by the streaming receiver was recorded every second, as part of the iperf3 command. The slice and \acs{UE} were also monitored at one-second intervals by the testbed passive monitoring services, as this is the Prometheus scrape interval for the exporter.

Approximately halfway through the transmission, a 4~Mbps uplink and downlink \acs{AMBR} was requested for the \acs{IMSI} of the modem directly to the Slice Manager \acs{API}, using the same underlying request that would be issued by the \acs{NEF} in the \textit{AsSessionWithQoS} implementation, as described in Section~\ref{chap:impl:exposure:nef:AsSessionWithQoS}. The request timestamp was recorded, and the operation identifier returned by the Slice Manager was used to retrieve the operation’s final state and determine the time required to apply the new \acs{QoS}. By comparing the measured throughput before and after this point, the experiment can evaluate whether the requested limit was enforced and how quickly the network responded to the change.

The second experiment extends the first approach to the application level, assessing whether, beyond altering the throughput, the resulting deprecated network conditions can also be observed in the behavior of the application, namely of the streaming session. In this experiment, the \acs{UE} streamed the 4K video at 30~\acs{FPS} to the destination \acs{VM}, which recorded the number of video frames received per second. In parallel, the network application subscribed to the AnalyticsExposure \acs{API} to monitor the throughput of the \acs{PPDR} camera stream. Throughput reports were received every 5s and evaluated against the 15~Mbps minimum requirement. The results presented in this section therefore focus exclusively on the \acs{NEF} interaction model, although the feedback loop was validated for all interaction models.

During these tests, the author was constrained by the availability of a single \acs{UE}, i.e., a \acs{5G} modem. This limitation required choosing between two mutually exclusive approaches. Either the scenario would consider only (i) the \acs{PPDR} video stream, imposing a predefined throughput cap from the start and interacting with the \acs{NEF} to request a \acs{QoS} upgrade to 15~Mbps to support the stream and improve its quality, or (ii) consider the civilian video stream, i.e. interacting with the \acs{NEF} to request a \acs{QoS} downgrade, its negative impact on the stream's performance. Both approaches, however, serve the same ultimate purpose, i.e. to demonstrate and validate the testbed network exposure interfaces can be used to dynamically prioritize \acs{PPDR} traffic over civilian traffic when network resources are limited. The latter approach was selected because it better aligns with the network application's value proposition. Rather than imposing a fixed throughput limit in advance, the network application monitors network conditions through the exposed interfaces and, once a degraded-throughput or emergency situation is detected, reduces the bandwidth consumption of non-critical traffic through adaptive \acs{QoS} degradation.

When a violation was detected, the application created a subscription through the AsSessionWithQoS \acs{API}, requesting the downgrade profile for the civilian \acs{UE}. The \acs{NEF} translated this request into the corresponding Slice Manager \acs{API} call and monitored the resulting operation until completion.

In this experiment, the \acs{PPDR} camera was not actively streaming. Consequently, the AnalyticsExposure \acs{API} reported a throughput of 0~bps for the camera, which the application treats as a requirements violation. This highlights the importance of jointly considering and correlating the state of the network, i.e. throughput reported by the \textit{AnalyticsExposure} \acs{API}, and the state of the stream, i.e. the stream current \acs{FPS}. This approach is outside the scope of the current solution, as the \acs{ML} crash detection was offloaded to a separate service, namely Roboflow. Nonetheless, for the purpose of this experiment, this behaviour was advantageous, as it triggered a scenario in which the \acs{PPDR} requirements were not being met and deterministically triggered the \acs{QoS} adjustment loop under evaluation.

The timestamps at which the application issued the request, the \acs{NEF} forwarded it to the Slice Manager, and the Slice Manager completed the operation were extracted from the application and \acs{NEF} logs. These events were correlated with the frame reception rate to assess the impact of the downgrade on the video stream. The frame rate was expected to remain close to 30~\acs{FPS} before the downgrade and decrease once the new \acs{QoS} configuration was enforced. Meanwhile, the testbed throughput dashboards were used to verify the corresponding changes at the network level, while the \acs{RTT} metrics measured by the active prober provided an additional indication of the slice's condition throughout the experiment.

The clocks of the OpenSlice \acs{VM}, hosting the K3s cluster with the exposure services and the network application, and of the stream destination \acs{VM} were synchronized through \acs{NTP} (server \texttt{ntp.ubuntu.com}), both reporting offsets below 4~ms. All timestamps and durations were therefore computed exclusively from these two hosts, with the Slice Manager operations accounted for through the durations it computes internally and reports via its \acs{API}.

\subsection{Results and Discussion}
\label{chap:results:use-case:discussion}

The results are presented according to the three aspects defined at the beginning of this section: the time required to enforce a new \acs{QoS} profile, the conformity of the observed network conditions with the network slice and the enforced profile, and the impact of the change on the \acs{QoE} of the civilian user, i.e. the stream \acs{FPS}.

The first experiment evaluates the first two aspects at the network level, establishing a baseline for the behavior of the Slice Manager and the \acs{5G} network. As previously established, the network slice and \acs{UE} are configured with a minimum uplink throughput of 15~Mbps and a maximum of 20~Mbps. The experiment then assesses whether the network slice and \acs{UE} association can sustain the iperf load under each \acs{QoS} profile. Figure~\ref{fig:results:use-case:test1:iperf} presents the offered~(client) and received~(server) throughput over time throughout this experiment. Table~\ref{tab:results:use-case:test1:iperf} complements this figure by summarizing the bitrate, datagram loss, and jitter measured with the iperf tool before and after the \acs{QoS} update.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figs/results/test1_throughput_iperf.pdf}
    \caption{Throughput received from the \acs{UE} before and after \acs{QoS} update}
    \label{fig:results:use-case:test1:iperf}
\end{figure}

Before the \acs{QoS} update, the received throughput closely matched the offered load of 15~Mbps, demonstrating that the network could sustain the guaranteed minimum uplink rate per \acs{UE} configured for the network slice. Individual measurements deviated only slightly from this value, ranging between 14.9 and 15.2~Mbps, with an average throughput equal to the offered load over the entire interval. During this period, no datagrams were lost, and jitter remained close to 1~ms. The \acs{QoS} update was requested at 13:22:03.454, approximately 60~s into the experiment. The first report following its enforcement showed a reduced throughput of 10.8~Mbps, with 27\% of datagrams lost. Since each report is obtained at one-second intervals, this interval still includes traffic received before the update. From then onwards, the received throughput remained between 3.8 and 4.0~Mbps until the end of the transmission, closely matching the requested 4~Mbps limit. The small difference to the nominal limit is consistent with iperf reporting the \acs{UDP} payload rate, whereas the \acs{AMBR} limit is enforced on the complete \acs{IP} packets. Since the \acs{UE} continued generating traffic at an offered rate of 15~Mbps, approximately 74\% of datagrams were dropped by the \acs{5G} network following the update, with jitter increasing to values between 3 and 5~ms.

The \acs{QoS} was enforced 67~ms after the request, with the \acs{CN} taking 54~ms to apply the updated \acs{AMBR} to the \acs{PDU} session. The same transition can also be observed from the metrics collected by the testbed's passive monitoring exporter. Figure~\ref{fig:results:use-case:test1:grafana} shows the Grafana throughput dashboard during the first experiment.

The uplink chart for the \acs{UE} closely resembles the traffic pattern observed in Figure~\ref{fig:results:use-case:test1:iperf}. This essentially validates the passive monitoring mechanism implemented in the testbed, confirming that it accurately captures actual network conditions without interfering with ongoing traffic. Nonetheless, some other details are important to point out.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figs/results/test1_throughput_grafana.png}
    \caption{Grafana Slice and per-\acs{UE} throughput dashboard during the first experiment}
    \label{fig:results:use-case:test1:grafana}
\end{figure}

The dashboard computes the throughput over a 2~s rate window, effectively providing an instantaneous rate between the two most recent byte samples scraped at 1~s intervals. In line with what was observed previously, the initial throughput remained at approximately 15~Mbps, before dropping to approximately 4~Mbps after the update. The mean values reported in each \acs{QoS} phase, of 15.3 and 4~Mbps respectively, are slightly higher than those reported by iperf, as the exporter counts the bytes of the complete Ethernet frame, including packet headers.

Additionally, the passive monitoring pipeline captured downlink traffic from the two \acs{VM} involved in the experiment: the iperf3 server (10.255.28.183) and the OpenSlice and K3s cluster (10.255.28.207). Two \acs{SSH} sessions were active on the \acs{UE} computer, but their traffic was negligible In addition, the previous Grafana dashboard hosted on the OpenSlice~\acs{VM} was continuously monitored in a browser, refreshing every 10~s. This resulted in the dashboards exhibiting peaks of downlink traffic from the OpenSlice \acs{VM} of approximately 420~kbps at 10~s intervals, corresponding to the dashboard refreshes. Following the transition, the periodic peaks were replaced by smaller, irregular bursts. This behaviour is consistent with uplink saturation, as browser requests and \acs{TCP} acknowledgements competed with the 15~Mbps offered by iperf3 for the available 4~Mbps, delaying dashboard responses and spreading the resulting downlink traffic over time. Once the transmission ended, the uplink was no longer saturated and the last refresh registered a peak of approximately 420~kbps again. This analysis also shows the usefulness of the per-\acs{IP} granularity of the monitoring exporter, allowing experimenters to identify unintended traffic that could otherwise bias the results of a test.

The results of the first experiment demonstrate that the \acs{QoS} of a \acs{UE} can be modified on demand, producing a measurable effect on the network. They also confirm that the network conditions observed by the testbed reflect the requested and enforced \acs{QoS} adjustments, confirming that the network can be both dynamically configured and monitored.

The second experiment will now address how the developed network interfaces can be used to change network conditions and affect the behavior of user applications. In this experiment, instead of interacting directly with the Slice Manager, it is the \acs{PPDR} network application that, in response to an analytics threshold breach, requests the \acs{QoS} change through the \acs{NEF}. The application and \acs{NEF} logs allow the sequence of interactions between the network application, the \acs{NEF} and the Slice Manager to be reconstructed. The network application created the \textit{AnalyticsExposure} subscription for the \acs{PPDR} camera at 14:46:06.503, in a request that took 64~ms. The first notification was received at 14:46:06.633 reporting 0~bps for the camera, indicating a violation of the defined performance threshold. Following this, the application requested the downgrade profile for the civilian \acs{UE} at 14:46:06.672, and the \acs{NEF} created the corresponding \textit{AsSessionWithQoS} subscription in 28~ms. The \acs{NEF} forwarded the request to the Slice Manager at 14:46:06.774, which accepted it in 48~ms and completed the operation in 129~ms, of which 91~ms in the \acs{CN}. The new \acs{QoS} profile was therefore enforced 259~ms after the request of the network application, and 298~ms after the violation was detected. This value is well within the 1~s defined in requirement~\ref{enum:nfr:qos-enforcement}. Compared with the first experiment, the exposure layer added approximately 100~ms before the request reached the Slice Manager, while the operation in the Slice Manager itself took 62~ms longer.

Figure~\ref{fig:results:use-case:test2:fps} presents the \acs{FPS} for the aforementioned \acs{UE}'s streaming application, representing non-critical traffic that should be restricted in emergency situations with the designed \acs{QoS} downgrade profile.

In the first half of the chart, the consumer frame rate remains relatively stable at about 30~fps, matching the target frame rate for the initial \acs{QoS} requirements, with occasional fluctuations between 23 and 37~\acs{FPS}. These variations may arise from several factors. Nonetheless, they predominantly occur in consecutive samples, which is consistent with the bursty delivery of frames over \acs{RTSP} and \acs{TCP} rather than with actual frame loss. Immediately after the \acs{QoS} change, the frame rate dropped to 8~\acs{FPS} and subsequently stabilized between 9 and 12~\acs{FPS}, with an average close to 11~\acs{FPS}, until the end of the stream. The restriction therefore noticeably degraded the \acs{QoE} of the civilian user without interrupting the service, demonstrating the intended trade-off between civilian and \acs{PPDR} communications, sharing the same underlying resources.

Once the \acs{QoS} adjustment was enforced on the network, its effect on the civilian \acs{UE} was expected to mirror the one observed in the first experiment, now under real application traffic, i.e. of the stream. Figure~\ref{fig:results:use-case:test2:grafana} confirms this behavior at the network level. The per-destination panels attribute the majority of the uplink traffic of the civilian \acs{UE} to the streaming receiver (10.255.28.183). The traffic curve follows the same trend observed in the first experiment.

Apart from an initial peak of 24.9~Mbps when the stream started, the uplink throughput of the civilian \acs{UE} fluctuated periodically (7~s video looping) around 14 to 15~Mbps, in line with the average bitrate of the video file. After the \acs{QoS} change, the uplink throughput decreased to approximately 4~Mbps, returning to zero when the stream stopped. The downlink direction is considerably lower, corresponding to mainly control and feedback data. The same effect is also visible in the downlink traffic, which decreased once the \acs{QoS} was restricted.

Lastly, active monitoring metrics provide an additional perspective on the impact of the civilian traffic on the slice. The \acs{ICMP} prober ordered for the slice runs as a pod on the K3s cluster hosted on the OpenSlice \acs{VM} and periodically sends echo requests to the civilian \acs{UE}. As specified in the service order, the prober sends a burst of six echo requests each 5 seconds, each with a 56~byte payload and a timeout of 800~ms. Since these probes traverse the same \acs{5G} network as the video stream, the measured \acs{RTT} provides an indication of the network conditions experienced by the civilian traffic. Figure~\ref{fig:results:use-case:test2:prober} presents the resulting prober dashboard.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figs/results/test2_prober.png}
    \caption{\acs{ICMP} prober dashboard during the second experiment}
    \label{fig:results:use-case:test2:prober}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:results:use-case:test2:prober}, the \acs{RTT} towards the civilian \acs{UE} was considerably higher while the stream was running, with a mean \acs{RTT} varying between 20 and 29~ms. Packet loss on the echo requests was also registered only while the stream was running, reaching a maximum of 6.25\%. Once the stream was stopped, the mean \acs{RTT} stabilized at approximately 11~ms. 

The latency observed under load thus exceeded the 20~ms defined in the \acs{NEST}. However, from the current data it's not possible to assume that is due to the best-effort behavior of the network. However, the \acs{RTT} did not decrease noticeably after the \acs{QoS} downgrade, but only after the stream was stopped. For instance, one possible explanation would be that the \acs{UE} itself delayed the echo replies while simultaneously transmitting the video stream. This would also explain why the \acs{RTT} did not decrease after the \acs{QoS} downgrade, but only after the stream was stopped. Nevertheless, these results show that civilian traffic affects not only the throughput but also the latency experienced on the shared resources, which reinforces the need to restrict it when \acs{PPDR} communications must be prioritized.

\subsection{Conclusions}
\label{chap:results:use-case:conclusion}

The experiments presented in this section demonstrate that the exposure interfaces developed in this dissertation enable on-demand adaptation of \acs{QoS} sessions in a live \acs{5G} network, with measurable effects on both network performance and the applications using it. 

For network applications such as the one developed in this dissertation, the results show that the network is no longer a static resource, but rather a dynamic infrastructure that adapts to application requirements through standardized interfaces, using the \acs{NEF} as the entrypoint to \acs{5G} network programmability. This enables civilian users to benefit from the network's full capacity under normal operating conditions, while ensuring that \acs{PPDR} communications receive additional resources whenever a \acs{PPDR} event occurs or their normal operation~is~impaired.
