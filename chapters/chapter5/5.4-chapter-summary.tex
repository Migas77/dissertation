\section{Chapter Summary}
\label{chap:results:summary}

This chapter validated the testbed by deploying and evaluating the developed network application throughout the successive stages of an experiment, from the provisioning of the requested services to the use of the network exposure interfaces in a live \acs{5G} network. Three experiments were conducted on the 5GAIner infrastructure, each addressing a different stage of this lifecycle.

Section~\ref{chap:results:delivery} presents the first experiment. This experiment measured the time required to provision a full network experiment, encompassing the network slice, the \acs{UE} association, the monitoring stack, the exposure interfaces and the network application, through a single service order submitted to OpenSlice. Results demonstrated that a vertical experimenter can self provision the environment consistently, without interacting with the underlying infrastructure. They also demonstrated that the largest share of the orchestration overhead was attributed to the periodic evaluation of \acs{LCM} rules, required for the sequential provisioning of the \acs{TMF} services.

The second experiment, presented in Section~\ref{chap:results:exposure}, measured and compared the response times of the operations supported by the \acs{NEF} and CAMARA \acsp{API}, as experienced by the network application. Both exposure models returned equivalent information, with the CAMARA abstraction layer introducing a small and consistent overhead in exchange for simpler, developer-facing interfaces. The discussion further highlighted the limitations of the evaluated CAMARA \acsp{API} in exchange for this simplicity. Since the testbed offers both interfaces, the choice between them is left to the application developer, according to the requirements of the vertical application.

Finally, Section~\ref{chap:results:use-case} combined the use of these capabilities in the \acs{E2E} adaptive \acs{QoS} scenario of the multi-vertical use case. The first test in this experiment demonstrated that the \acs{QoS} of a \acs{UE} can be dynamically adjusted in a live network, with the requested uplink limit being promptly enforced and accurately reflected by the testbed's passive monitoring mechanisms. In order to evaluate the impact of such adjustments on application behavior, the full adaptive \acs{QoS} loop was exercised in the second test using the developed network application. Through the \acs{NEF} and CAMARA \acsp{API}, the network application monitored the throughput of the \acs{PPDR} stream and, once a violation of its requirements was reported, requested a \acs{QoS} downgrade for the civilian \acs{UE}. As a result, the new \acs{QoS} profile degraded the frame rate delivered by the civilian video stream, showcasing how the network application can leverage the capabilities provided by the network to dynamically prioritize \acs{PPDR} communications over civilian traffic competing for the same resources.
